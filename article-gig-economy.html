Labor

The gig economy isn't freedom. It's a benefits problem with a marketing budget.

Tobias Velez·October 2025·6 min read

Uber, DoorDash, Fiverr, TaskRabbit — at some point all of these started using the word "freedom" in their advertising. Be your own boss. Set your own hours. Own your time. And the pitch works, at least aesthetically. Especially if you're in high school or college and the alternative is a fixed schedule at a retail job, the gig model sounds genuinely better.

But I kept running into people who had been doing it for a while and didn't seem to feel free. A cousin who delivers for DoorDash and Uber Eats simultaneously to make anything close to a living wage. A family friend who does freelance design work and spends a non-trivial portion of her time on administrative stuff — invoicing, chasing late payments, keeping her own books — for which she earns nothing. The freedom pitch started to seem like it was describing something real but leaving out a lot of the math.

So I tried to figure out what gig work actually costs compared to traditional employment, in dollar terms. The numbers are bigger than I expected.

What a W-2 employee actually gets beyond the paycheck

When you work a regular job, your employer has to provide certain things by law, and a competitive employer provides additional things to attract workers. Most people don't think of these as compensation because they don't show up as cash in your account, but they are. They're just invisible until you don't have them.

Health insurance is the obvious one. Employer-sponsored plans typically cover 70 to 80% of the premium. For a mid-tier plan in 2025, the full premium runs around $8,400 a year for a single person. Your employer covers $6,000 of that and you pay $2,400. As a gig worker buying that same plan through the ACA marketplace without subsidies, you pay the full $8,400. That's a $6,000 annual compensation difference that never shows up when you compare hourly rates.

Social Security and Medicare work the same way. A traditional employee pays 7.65% of their wages in FICA taxes and their employer matches it. A self-employed gig worker — which is your legal classification as an independent contractor — pays both sides. That's 15.3% of all net self-employment income. On $40,000 of gig earnings, that's $6,120 going to payroll taxes versus $3,060 for someone at a traditional job making the same amount.

If a Lyft driver tells you they make $22 an hour, that's before subtracting: the self-employment tax premium (about $1.08/hr on that wage), vehicle depreciation (IRS standard mileage is $0.67/mile, and you cover a lot of miles), gas, phone data, and time spent waiting between rides that the app doesn't compensate. The effective hourly rate is often meaningfully lower than the number that gets quoted.

The retirement situation

This is the one that bothers me most. At a traditional job, many employers offer a 401k with a match — often 3 to 5% of your salary. On $40,000 that's $1,200 to $2,000 per year that your employer contributes to your retirement that you don't have to. As a gig worker you can open a SEP-IRA or solo 401k and contribute your own money, but the employer match doesn't exist. Nobody is matching your contributions. You're funding both sides of your retirement entirely on your own.

Over 40 years, a 4% employer match at $40,000 annual income is about $1,600 per year. Compounded at 7% over 40 years that's roughly $340,000. Nobody talks about the gig economy as costing you $340,000 in retirement contributions, but that's what the math says.

Same gross income. The traditional employee gets $9,200 in employer-paid benefits (health insurance share, FICA match, retirement match). The gig worker pays $15,300 in costs that don't show up in their advertised rate: extra FICA burden, full insurance premium, no retirement match, vehicle depreciation.

When gig work actually makes sense

I don't want to make it sound like the gig economy is just a scam. It fills a real function. For someone who is a student, has a second job with benefits, or needs temporary income with maximum schedule flexibility, the trade-off can work. The problem is that a growing number of people are using it as their primary income and primary source of economic stability, without fully accounting for what they're giving up. The platforms benefit from that misunderstanding. The "freedom" framing is doing a lot of work to obscure a fairly unfavorable total compensation calculation.

When gig work makes sense

  • You already have health insurance elsewhere
  • You have a traditional job with retirement benefits
  • You genuinely need schedule flexibility above all else
  • It's supplemental income, not your only income

When it quietly costs you

  • It's your primary income and you have no other benefits
  • You're not setting aside 15.3% for self-employment tax
  • You're not accounting for vehicle depreciation or expenses
  • You're not contributing to retirement on your own

What the policy argument is actually about

There's an ongoing legal debate in a lot of states about whether gig workers should be reclassified as employees rather than independent contractors. California passed Prop 22 in 2020 creating a middle category with some benefits. The platforms spent something like $200 million to pass it, which tells you something about how valuable the contractor classification is to their business model. If they had to provide health insurance and payroll tax matches to all their workers, the economics change significantly.

The argument for reclassification is basically what I've been laying out: the total compensation picture for full-time gig workers is worse than it looks on paper, and the structure shifts costs onto workers that traditional employers absorb. The argument against is that reclassification would reduce flexibility and might reduce total employment by making it more expensive to hire. Both of those things are probably partially true. The question is which trade-off you prefer — and that depends a lot on whether you're a platform, a worker who needs the flexibility, or a worker who has no other options and is absorbing costs they don't fully see.
