March 2026 macro review: stability under pressure

Tobias Velez·March 2026·6 min read

The honest description of the U.S. economy right now is that it's fine, in the way that "fine" sometimes means genuinely okay and sometimes means we're not dealing with the problems that are building up underneath. I've been trying to figure out which one applies here. After going through the March data I think it's somewhere in between, which is not a satisfying answer but is probably the accurate one.

Inflation is down. The Fed hasn't moved rates in months. Growth is still positive. On paper, that sounds like a soft landing — the thing everyone was hoping for after 2022. In practice, it feels more like everything is holding together with less margin than it looks like from the headline numbers.

Inflation: the gap between the number and how it feels

February CPI came in at 2.4% year-over-year. That's close to the Fed's 2% target, and if you only look at the headline it seems like the inflation problem is basically solved. The issue is that 2.4% is an average across a huge basket of goods and services, and the things inside that basket are not all behaving the same way.

Shelter costs are still running well above overall CPI. Food is drifting up slowly. Energy spiked again in February and those increases are starting to show up at gas stations. The categories that are dragging the headline down are things like used cars and some electronics, which have deflated after the 2021-2022 pandemic price surge. So the number is real, but the 2.4% experience is pretty unevenly distributed depending on what you actually spend money on.

This is why a lot of people I know — adults, people's parents — still feel like inflation is a problem even when the data says it's mostly under control. Rent, groceries, gas: those are things you encounter constantly. Used car prices coming down matters a lot less if you're not buying a car right now.

The rate held flat at 3.75% while CPI drifted down slowly. That gap — rates well above inflation — is the definition of "restrictive" monetary policy. It's deliberate. The Fed wants rates to stay above inflation to keep cooling things down, and it's working, slowly.

The Fed is not moving and the reason is actually reasonable

At their March meeting, the Fed held rates at 3.5 to 3.75%. They've been at or near this level since mid-2024. The reason they're not cutting yet comes down to one word: confidence. They don't have enough of it that inflation won't bounce back up if they ease. And energy prices moving again, plus the uncertainty around tariffs potentially re-inflating goods prices, makes them more cautious not less.

I've been reading Fed press conference transcripts for this newsletter and Powell's language is genuinely careful to the point of being almost frustrating. He says things like "data dependent" and "attentive to risks on both sides" which roughly translates to: we're watching and we're not committing to anything until we're sure. Markets are pricing in maybe one small cut in late 2026, and only if both inflation and the labor market soften further. The era of 0% interest rates is over and is probably over for years, not months.

The main thing I take from this table is that the U.S. is actually doing relatively well compared to Europe and Japan, which are growing even more slowly with similar inflation dynamics. The places growing fast are emerging markets — India, parts of Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa — but they're dealing with higher inflation and more exposure to energy price swings. If oil goes up significantly and stays up, those countries face a choice between tolerating higher inflation or tightening rates into economic weakness. Neither is good. That's the main global risk I'm watching.

What this means if you're a high schooler thinking about any of this

Rates staying elevated means borrowing is expensive. Student loans, car loans, mortgages — all of that costs meaningfully more than it did three years ago, and there's no clear timeline for when it gets better. If you're taking on debt soon, that's worth actually pricing in rather than assuming rates will come down before it matters to you.

The local connection: everything I've written about Bethesda housing and the lock-in effect is directly tied to rates staying where they are. Nobody with a 3% mortgage is selling at 3.75% federal funds rate, which means 6.5-7% mortgage rates, which means the inventory problem doesn't resolve. These numbers aren't abstract. They're connected to real things happening in the places we live.
