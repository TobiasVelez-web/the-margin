April check-in: tariffs broke something and I don't think anyone knows how bad yet

Tobias Velez·April 2026·7 min read

My AP Macro teacher showed us the "Liberation Day" tariff announcement in class and asked us what we thought would happen. Most people said prices go up. A few said nothing because companies absorb it. I said I genuinely didn't know, which felt like the honest answer. That was two weeks ago. Since then the stock market has had one of its worst stretches since 2022 and the Fed is quietly signaling it might have to choose between fighting inflation and preventing a recession at the same time. That's the thing it's been trying hardest to avoid for three years.

So here's what I actually understand about where April left the economy, and where I'm still confused.

What just happened with tariffs

On April 2nd, the administration announced what got labeled "Liberation Day" — a sweeping new tariff structure hitting imports from basically every major trading partner. The headline number was a 10% baseline on almost everything, with significantly higher rates on specific countries: 145% on Chinese goods, 25% on Canada and Mexico. The logic offered was reciprocity — we're matching what other countries charge us. Economists spent the next 48 hours explaining why the math used to calculate those rates was wrong, but that's a separate piece.

What matters for this month's review is what happened next. Markets dropped 10% in four days. The White House paused most of the tariffs for 90 days on April 9th — except China, which stayed at 145%. China retaliated with 125% tariffs on American goods. So the two largest economies in the world are now effectively in a trade embargo with each other while the rest of the tariffs are in a pause that might or might not become permanent.

A 145% tariff on Chinese goods doesn't mean things get 145% more expensive. It means importing from China becomes economically irrational for most products, so companies either find other suppliers or stop selling certain things. The disruption comes from the supply chain chaos, not just the price increase.

What it's doing to inflation

March CPI came in at 2.4%, basically flat from February. That's the last clean number we're going to get for a while. The tariffs announced in April haven't hit consumer prices yet — there's usually a 60 to 90 day lag between a tariff going up and the price change showing up in the data. Which means May and June CPI are going to be more interesting and probably worse.

The categories to watch are electronics, apparel, and anything that moves through a China-dependent supply chain. A lot of things you buy at Target or Walmart come from Chinese manufacturers. Even things assembled in America often have Chinese components. When 145% tariffs make those components uneconomical to import, companies either eat the cost, find alternatives fast, or raise prices. Most of them will raise prices.

The Fed's impossible position

Here's the problem the Fed did not want. Tariffs cause inflation — that's just prices going up because imported goods cost more. The normal response to inflation is raising interest rates. But tariffs also slow economic growth, because when trade gets disrupted, companies invest less, hire less, and consumer spending pulls back. The normal response to a slowing economy is cutting rates.

You can't do both at the same time. This is called stagflation — inflation and stagnation together — and it's what made the 1970s so economically painful and so politically ugly. The Fed held rates at 3.75% in April and basically said nothing useful at the press conference. Powell talked about "significant uncertainty" which is Fed language for "we're watching and hoping this resolves before we have to make a hard choice."

Markets were pricing in two or three rate cuts this year as recently as January. Now they're pricing in zero, maybe one, and there's actual probability being assigned to a rate hike. That's a significant reversal in four months.

GDP is still positive but the vibe is different

First quarter GDP came in at 2.3% annualized, which is fine. The problem is that a big chunk of that growth came from a pre-tariff import surge — companies rushing to buy foreign goods before the tariffs hit, pulling forward demand that won't repeat in Q2. Strip that out and underlying growth was closer to 1.5%. The Atlanta Fed's real-time GDP tracker, which I check more than I probably should, was showing Q2 growth at around 0.5% as of late April. That's not a recession but it's close enough to make people nervous.

What I think is actually going on

I keep going back to something my macro teacher said: the economy is a confidence machine as much as it's a production machine. Companies invest when they think the future is predictable enough to plan around. Consumers spend when they feel stable. What the April tariff situation did — more than any specific price increase — was make the next six months genuinely unpredictable. The 90-day pause means every business decision that depends on international supply chains is on hold until August. That pause in decision-making is its own kind of economic damage, and it doesn't show up cleanly in CPI or GDP.

I'll do a May update when the first post-tariff inflation numbers are out. That's when we'll actually know how much of this got passed to consumers and how much companies absorbed.
