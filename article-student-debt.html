Personal Finance

I'm about to apply to colleges and the student debt numbers are genuinely freaking me out. So I looked into them.

Tobias Velez·January 2026·7 min read

I've been filling out college stuff and at some point my parents sat me down to talk about finances. Not in a scary way, but in a "we need to be real with each other" way. My family isn't wealthy. There are schools I want to apply to that cost $85,000 a year. And the gap between what financial aid calculators say we'd get and what those schools actually cost is not small.

So I started actually researching student debt, partly because I needed to understand my own situation and partly because it felt like something worth writing about. There's $1.7 trillion of it in the United States. That number is so large it's basically meaningless until you break it down into what it actually looks like for individual people.

The numbers are worse than the headline

The average student loan debt for a bachelor's degree graduate is around $37,000. That sounds manageable until you look at the distribution. The average is pulled up by graduate and professional students — law school, med school, MBA programs — who borrow much more. For undergrads specifically, about half graduate with less than $30,000. But about a quarter graduate with more than $50,000, and a meaningful number are over $100,000. The average hides the fact that the people being most damaged by this aren't the average borrower.

The other thing the average hides: what major you chose and what the job market for it looks like. $37,000 in debt from a nursing degree, where starting salaries run $65,000 to $75,000, is a very different situation from $37,000 in debt from a degree in a field where entry-level work is scarce or underpaid. The debt amount is the same. The math is completely different.

Starting salary vs. average debt by field (approximate, 2025)
Same debt, very different math. A CS grad earning $95k with $40k in debt has a manageable ratio. A Fine Arts grad earning $38k with $45k in debt has a problem that takes years to dig out of.

How the interest turns a $37k problem into a much bigger one

Federal student loan rates right now are around 6.5% for undergraduates. On $37,000 that's $2,405 in interest in the first year alone, before you've paid down a single dollar of principal. The standard repayment plan is 10 years. At 6.5% on $37,000 over 10 years, you end up paying back about $50,000 total — $13,000 more than you borrowed.

For people on income-driven repayment plans, which cap your monthly payment as a percentage of your income, the problem is that if your income is low enough, your monthly payment might not even cover the interest. So the balance grows while you're making payments. I've talked to people in their 30s who have been paying on their loans for 8 years and owe more than when they graduated. That is a real outcome for a meaningful number of borrowers, not an edge case.

The forgiveness debate is real and complicated, but I think the more important question is why we designed a system where this is possible at all. A loan structure where you can make on-time payments for years and end up deeper in debt than when you started is not a well-designed loan structure. That's the thing that seems most worth fixing regardless of where you land politically on broad forgiveness.

What I'm actually doing with this information

I've started treating college affordability as part of the decision, not a separate financial aid question to figure out after I decide where I want to go. A school I genuinely want to attend that costs $85,000 a year with a $30,000 aid package is $55,000 a year out of pocket. Four years of that is $220,000. That's not a normal debt load for someone who wants to study economics and maybe go into policy or research — fields that don't start at $120,000 a year.

The thing that would have helped me most a year ago is understanding the difference between "cost of attendance" and "net price after financial aid" and then understanding that merit aid varies wildly between schools with similar rankings. A school ranked 40th might give you significantly more merit money than a school ranked 20th, and four years later you've got similar credentials and $80,000 less debt. That trade-off is worth actually calculating rather than assuming prestige pays for itself. Sometimes it does. Often it doesn't. The data on earnings by school, once you control for what students majored in, is a lot less dramatic than the rankings suggest.
