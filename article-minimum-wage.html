Labor

everyone has an opinion on the minimum wage. most of them are wrong about something.

Tobias Velez·February 2026·6 min read

I make $15 an hour at the camp where I work summers. Maryland's minimum wage is $15. So technically I'm making minimum wage, which felt weird to process when I first started. Not because I feel underpaid — for a high schooler working with kids that's actually fine — but because I started thinking about what it would mean to actually live on that. Like, not as a summer job. As your whole income.

$15 an hour, 40 hours a week, is $31,200 a year before taxes. In Bethesda, the cheapest one-bedroom apartment I could find on Zillow last week was $2,100 a month. That's $25,200 a year just in rent, before utilities, food, or literally anything else. The math doesn't work. And that made me want to actually understand the minimum wage debate instead of just having a vague feeling about it.

The argument against raising it — the real version, not the strawman

The concern economists have traditionally raised is simple: if you force employers to pay more, some of them will hire fewer people. You price some workers out of jobs they would have had at the lower wage. Introductory economics textbooks have a supply-demand diagram showing exactly this — set a price floor above the market equilibrium and you get a surplus, in this case a surplus of labor (unemployment) because the quantity of labor demanded falls.

That's the theory. For a long time the empirical research more or less supported it, especially for large, fast increases. A business that was barely profitable at $8/hour minimum wage might not be able to absorb $15/hour without cutting hours or positions.

But then Seattle happened, and economists got confused

In 2015 Seattle raised its minimum wage to $13 and eventually $15. Researchers at the University of Washington studied it and initially found significant job losses — their 2017 paper got a ton of attention. Then other researchers looked at the same data differently and found much smaller effects. Then more cities raised their minimums and economists started tracking all of them.

What the broader evidence ended up showing is messier than the textbook. Employment effects from moderate minimum wage increases — say 10 to 20% above the current level — are small and sometimes statistically undetectable. The industries that employ the most minimum wage workers, food service and retail, didn't collapse in the cities that raised wages. In some cases turnover went down (keeping workers costs less than constantly training new ones) which partially offset the higher wage cost for employers.

The most interesting finding from the Seattle research wasn't about jobs at all — it was about hours. Some employers responded not by cutting workers but by cutting hours. So the same number of people had jobs, but their total earnings barely changed. A higher hourly rate doesn't help if you're working fewer hours.

The federal minimum wage is $7.25, which is insane

It hasn't been raised since 2009. That's 17 years. Adjusted for inflation, $7.25 in 2026 is worth less than the minimum wage was in 1970. We've had 17 years of inflation, 17 years of productivity growth, 17 years of the economy changing, and the floor hasn't moved.

Federal minimum wage: nominal vs. inflation-adjusted (2026 dollars)
The nominal wage (red) has been frozen at $7.25 since 2009. Its real value (gray, adjusted for inflation) has been quietly shrinking the whole time. It's now lower in real terms than it was in 1970.

The "one national number" problem

Here's the part that actually convinced me the debate is more nuanced than either side usually admits. A $15 federal minimum wage makes total sense in Bethesda, where even that doesn't cover rent. It might genuinely cause job losses in rural Mississippi, where $15 could be 80% of median wages and most businesses are running on thin margins. The cost of living difference between those two places is enormous, but a federal minimum wage has to be one number.

That's probably the strongest argument for letting states and cities set their own minimums, which is basically what's been happening since Congress stopped moving. Maryland is at $15. California pushed past it. Alabama is still at $7.25 because it has no state minimum. Whether that's a feature or a bug depends on how you think about federalism versus baseline economic protection for workers.

Where I land

I think the federal minimum wage should be higher than $7.25 and indexed to inflation so it doesn't need to be a political fight every decade. The research on job losses from moderate increases is genuinely mixed — not as catastrophic as critics claim, not as harmless as proponents claim. The housing math I described at the top doesn't work at $7.25 anywhere in America and doesn't really work at $15 in most cities either. That gap between what the floor is and what housing actually costs is the part that seems most broken to me. Whether the fix is a higher minimum wage, housing policy, or both is something I genuinely don't know — but pretending $7.25 is still a reasonable floor in 2026 seems like the least defensible position in the whole debate.
