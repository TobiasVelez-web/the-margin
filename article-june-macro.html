June 2026 macro update: the truce nobody trusts

Tobias Velez·June 2026·7 min read

In May, the U.S. and China announced a 90-day pause on their escalating tariff war. The Dow jumped 1,100 points in a single day. Headlines called it a breakthrough. And then, pretty quickly, people started reading the actual details and the enthusiasm got quieter.

The pause reduced U.S. tariffs on Chinese goods from 145% to 30%, and China cut its retaliatory tariffs from 125% to 10%. Those are real reductions. But 30% is still historically very high. The underlying disputes over technology transfer, subsidies, and market access — the things that actually started this — weren't touched. What was agreed to was a pause on escalation, not a resolution of anything. Those are different things, and markets eventually figured that out.

That's where the economy is right now. Officially stable. Quietly fragile.

What the numbers actually look like

CPI came in at 2.4% year-over-year for May. That's basically flat from where it was in March, which sounds fine until you look at what's inside the number. Services inflation, which covers things like rent, medical care, and eating out, is running closer to 3.8%. Goods prices are lower, partly because import costs dropped temporarily during the tariff negotiation period. If the truce falls apart and tariffs go back up, that goods deflation reverses pretty fast.

The Fed has left rates at 3.75% and basically said they're not moving until they have more confidence that inflation is sustainably at 2%, not just near it. Chair Powell's language in the last press conference was careful to the point of being almost deliberately unreadable. He acknowledged the uncertainty around trade policy and said the Fed "would be prepared to act in either direction." That's central bank for: we have no idea what happens next and we're not going to guess in public.

The Fed has held at 3.75% while inflation has drifted just above 2%. The gap between those two lines is why borrowing still feels expensive even though prices have slowed down significantly from the 2022 peaks.

The labor market is still holding, barely

May payrolls came in at 139,000 jobs added, which is below the 180,000 monthly average from the prior two years. Unemployment is at 4.1%, up from 3.8% in early 2026. That doesn't sound like much, but the direction matters. The unemployment rate had been flat for almost 18 months. Watching it start to tick up is different from watching it hold still.

What's driving it is mostly sectors that are sensitive to trade uncertainty: manufacturing, logistics, construction. Companies in those industries aren't mass laying people off, but they're not hiring to fill vacancies either. Hiring freezes are softer than layoffs but they still show up in the data eventually, and they're starting to.

The sectors adding jobs consistently right now are healthcare and government. Those are both real jobs that real people need. But they're also both essentially insulated from trade conditions. The fact that they're carrying the jobs numbers while trade-exposed sectors stall is part of why the headline unemployment rate and the broader economic anxiety don't feel like they match.

What the truce actually changed

The 90-day pause is real but narrow. Here's what it did: it reduced tariffs enough that supply chains can start to function again on some Chinese goods that had been effectively blocked by 145% duties. Electronics assembly, certain auto components, consumer goods. Companies that had been holding inventory decisions are starting to move again.

What it didn't do: resolve any of the underlying issues. The U.S. still has restrictions on selling advanced semiconductors to China. China still subsidizes its domestic industries in ways the U.S. considers unfair. Neither side has changed any of those policies. The 90 days is time to negotiate a longer-term framework, but trade negotiations that actually work tend to take years, not months. So the honest read is: we bought some time and we don't know what happens when it runs out.

GDP growth slowed more than expected

First quarter GDP came in at 1.4% annualized, which was revised down from the initial 1.6% estimate. The main drag was business investment — companies are holding off on big capital expenditures when they don't know what the trade environment looks like in six months. Consumer spending held up better than expected, which is what kept growth positive. But consumer spending is largely funded by wages and credit, and if the labor market softens further, that buffer starts to erode.

Two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth is the technical definition of a recession. We're not there. But we were at 2.9% growth in early 2025 and we're at 1.4% now. The trend line matters as much as the number, and the trend has been going in one direction for four straight quarters.

What this means practically

For anyone taking on debt right now — student loans, car loans, credit cards — rates are still high and they're not coming down soon. The Fed has been pretty consistent that it needs to see inflation sustainably at 2% and a stable labor market before it cuts. Right now inflation is near 2% but not quite there, and the labor market just started softening. That's not the combination that gets you a rate cut.

For the housing market specifically, the lock-in effect I wrote about in the Bethesda piece isn't going anywhere. Rates at 3.75% on the federal funds side still translate to mortgage rates around 6.5 to 7%. Nobody with a 3% mortgage is selling voluntarily in that environment. Inventory stays low, prices stay elevated, and the same structural problem keeps running.

The honest summary of June 2026 is this: the economy is still growing, just more slowly. The tariff situation got less bad but didn't get good. The Fed is stuck. And most of the uncertainty that's been hanging over everything since late 2025 is still there, just quieter for now. The 90 days will be up in August. I'll write about it then.
