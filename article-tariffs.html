How tariffs make everything more expensive — including your next car

Tobias Velez·March 2026·6 min read

My family was looking at cars earlier this year and the prices were genuinely confusing to me. Not just expensive — I expected that — but expensive in a way that didn't match what I knew about the used car market starting to cool down. We're talking about a 2023 Honda CR-V, nothing exotic, and the numbers had jumped noticeably even compared to six months earlier. When I started looking into it I found out tariffs were a significant part of the explanation, and once I understood the mechanism I started seeing it show up in things way beyond cars.

The thing that surprised me most wasn't the size of the price increases. It was who was actually paying for them. Because the way tariffs get talked about politically, you'd think China or Canada is paying some kind of fine. They're not. Mostly we are.

What a tariff actually does

A tariff is a tax collected at the border on imported goods. When the U.S. puts a 25% tariff on Canadian steel, a U.S. company importing that steel now pays 25% more for it. The Canadian steel company doesn't write a check to the U.S. Treasury. The American importer does, at the border, and then passes most of that cost down the supply chain until it lands in the price of whatever the steel ended up becoming — a car, a refrigerator, a building.

Researchers at the Peterson Institute looked at the 2018 tariffs and found that American consumers and businesses absorbed about 90% of the total cost. Foreign exporters absorbed roughly 10%, mostly through adjusting their prices slightly to stay competitive. The political message and the economic reality are almost perfectly inverted.

The framing is always "we're making China pay." The data is consistently "Americans pay 90% of it." I'm not saying tariffs are never worth it — there are real arguments for them in specific cases. But the "other country pays" premise is just not how the mechanism works, and it's worth understanding that before forming an opinion about the policy.

What's actually happening right now with the numbers

Starting in late 2025, the current administration put 25% tariffs on goods from Canada and Mexico — including steel, auto parts, and dairy — and layered on tariffs ranging from 10% to 145% on Chinese goods, hitting electronics and electric vehicles especially hard. The U.S. Trade Representative says this covers over $800 billion in imports.

The retaliation was fast. China put 20-50% tariffs on American soybeans, lithium batteries, and cars. Canada added 10% on U.S. alcohol and farm products. The EU targeted American cars and bourbon. When both sides are doing this, both sides' consumers end up paying more. That's the definition of a trade war — mutually assured economic inconvenience.

Cars and electronics include a lot of imported parts. Even something "assembled in America" might have components that crossed a border two or three times before getting here. The tariff hits each crossing.

Why cars specifically are getting hit hard

A typical American car has parts sourced from 10 or more countries. The engine components, the semiconductors in the dashboard, the steel in the frame — a lot of that crosses the U.S.-Canada-Mexico border multiple times during manufacturing under the old free trade rules that were designed to make that possible. When you put 25% tariffs on those crossings, you're taxing the same car's supply chain several times over.

Kelley Blue Book data puts the average new car price up about 8% from 2024 to early 2026. On a $35,000 car that's $2,800, most of which is effectively a tariff pass-through to the buyer. Automakers could absorb some of that cost, but they're choosing not to, and their margins tell you why — this isn't a situation where they were making enough profit to quietly eat a trade policy change. The cost lands on whoever is buying the car.

What happened the last time we tried this

The 2018-2019 tariffs were a real-world test. They covered about $360 billion in goods. The Peterson Institute found U.S. consumers and businesses paid about 90% of the costs, same as what current research suggests. China's exports to the U.S. did fall — by about 25% on targeted goods. But Vietnam and Mexico's exports to the U.S. jumped 30% to fill the gap, so most of the manufacturing that was supposed to "come home" just rerouted through different countries instead. And American farmers got hit hard by Chinese retaliation — soybean exports to China fell nearly 40%, which required an $8 billion federal bailout to offset. It's complicated to call that a win for American industry.

When they might actually make sense

I want to be fair to the other side of this because I think the strongest version of the pro-tariff argument is real. There are industries where the national security case is legitimate. Semiconductors are the clearest example right now — if the U.S. can't manufacture its own advanced chips and the supply chain runs through Taiwan, that's a genuine vulnerability. Targeted tariffs to protect or rebuild that capacity make more sense than broad consumer goods tariffs.

The problem is that "targeted and strategic" and "broad and politically popular" are different things, and policy tends to drift toward the second. A 25% tariff on Canadian dairy doesn't obviously strengthen American national security. It makes cheese more expensive and starts a retaliatory cycle that hurts American exporters. The case for that is harder to make than the semiconductor one, and I think it's worth being honest about the difference.
